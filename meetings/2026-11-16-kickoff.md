## Summary

### Brief constraints we talked about
- The brief asks for a **probability analysis**: define the events you're studying and estimate the relevant probabilities or conditional probabilities from the data. It is not asking for a forecasting model.
- The lecturer's Q&A says the core methods have to come from the module. Extra material is allowed, but the project is meant to show we've mastered the module content.
- The data should come mainly from **Met Éireann**.
- Any AI use has to be disclosed. Work that's clearly beyond the module will look like it was AI-generated.

### Ideas we considered and dropped
- **Weather predictor / mean-reverting OLS model:** this is what everyone else will do. It's building a model rather than analysing one, and a lot of effort to do by hand.
- **"Rain before 7, fine by 11"** (Dublin Airport: is P(dry at 11 | rain before 7) > P(dry at 11)?): a good conditional-probability framing, but too simple.
- **Rainfall in Dublin vs Cork:** a generic idea from ChatGPT.
- **Pressure gradients and major weather events** (e.g. Beast from the East), using a Bayesian approach across synoptic stations.
- **Can western stations give early warning of eastern weather?**
- **Wind data instead of rain:** a less popular choice that would stand out more.
- **Windiest place in Ireland:** dropped because it has a single answer and involves no probability.

### Agreed direction
**Use weather data to test the assumption a farmer's frost-warning model would rely on, rather than building the model itself.**

Working research question:
> Can early-spring weather conditions (grass minimum temperature, soil temperature, dew point, etc.) give Irish farmers a reliable early warning to delay planting and avoid damaging ground frost? Does the answer depend on where the farm is?

Why we chose it:
- It tests the underlying assumption, which is the first step before anyone builds a prediction model. If the assumption is false, a model built on it gives meaningless results.
- **We don't need to find a signal.** A well-justified null result is still a valid conclusion.
- It uses a niche dataset (grass minimum temperature) and has a relatable agricultural angle.
- It fits module content well: **Bayes' theorem, conditional probability, normal distributions, multiple continuous random variables, joint distributions and covariance.** We should use several variables so we don't leave marks on the table.

### Data notes
- **Grass minimum temperature:** the coldest overnight temperature just above the ground. The 09 UTC reading covers the previous night. It is recorded at climatological stations (about 56 of them).
- **Soil:** mean 10 cm soil temperature and soil moisture, from the climatological stations.
- **Dew point:** from the synoptic stations (about 20 in Ireland).
- Frost is not measured directly, but it can be inferred from its drivers, mainly ground temperature combined with dew point.
- **Season:** January to March, or late winter to spring, when crops are planted.
- **Time window:** about **2016–2026 (10 years)**.
  - Older data reflects a different climate.
  - A window that's too short risks missing longer cycles.
  - We need to justify choosing 10 years over 5 or 15.
  - One CSV we checked covers 1994–2016, so check what's actually available.
- **Locations:** focus on agricultural regions (Midlands, South, Tipperary, Kilkenny, Waterford, Cork and so on). Leave out urban stations and poor-land areas such as Mayo and Galway.
- **Data cleaning:** watch for poorly sited or faulty stations producing outliers.

### Weighting and dependence between stations
- The proposed approach is to weight stations by agricultural activity, for example farmland area from the CSO Census of Agriculture.
- Feedback from Claude:
  - March temperatures are highly correlated across stations (mean r ≈ 0.82, range 0.65–0.93), so a cold March tends to be cold everywhere at once.
  - Frost risk itself is very local (about 0.40 at Mullingar versus 0.16 at Valentia), so a single national average would mislead both coastal and Midland farms.
  - Because stations move together, 472 station-years of data hold only about 70 independent years of information.
- **Conclusion:** stations can't be treated as independent, so we need **covariance terms**. This also adds legitimate mathematical depth.
- **Idea:** a small function where a farmer enters their local station and gets its covariance with the weighted national signal. That shows how much the headline advice applies to their area, and it resolves the national-versus-local question.

### Assumptions and limitations (for the write-up)
- Older stations may be less accurate than modern ones.
- Climate is not stationary, which is why we restrict the time window.
- Station data is correlated in space, not independent.
- Advice applies differently depending on how heavily the farmer's region is weighted.
- We're analysing whether a signal exists, not producing a forecast. Note where the analysis breaks down and what methods beyond the module would be needed to fix it.

### Possible conclusions
- **No signal:** there is no significant evidence that farmers should change planting times based on these conditions, with an explanation of why.
- **Signal found:** farmers should adjust planting times, while taking into account their location and how relevant the weighted advice is to them.

### Work split (provisional)
- **Maths and assumptions:** the probabilistic method, the assumptions write-up, and the conclusion and how it would be applied. Taken on by a member who is less confident with code.
- **Code:** an R script. We agreed to write it in R from the start rather than translating from Python. Everyone expects this to be the easier part.
- Still to do: decide exactly which weather variables to use, and download the data.
